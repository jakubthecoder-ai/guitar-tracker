# Evidence-Based Practice Pedagogy for a Self-Taught Electric Guitarist (AI Practice Coach)

Evidence grading used throughout: **[STRONG]** = meta-analyses / replicated lab findings in motor learning; **[MODERATE]** = small music-specific experiments or meta-analyses with disputed interpretation; **[ANECDOTAL]** = educator opinion, forum reports, single case studies, or my unverified recall. **Guitar-specific experimental evidence is almost absent**; most music studies use clarinet, piano or woodwinds with small samples (n = 10–41).

---

## 1. Deliberate practice (Ericsson) and critiques: what applies to adult self-learners

### Takeaway
Structured, goal-directed, feedback-rich practice reliably matters, but it explains only part of skill differences (estimates ~14–36% of variance in music depending on definitions). For a coach: *how* the time is spent (targeted error correction) predicts progress better than raw minutes.

### Cited Findings
- Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, Psychological Science), 88 studies, >11,135 participants: deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, **21% in music**, 18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions. Conclusion: important but "not as important as has been argued." [MODERATE] — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140716123835.htm); [Princeton news](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2014/07/03/becoming-expert-takes-more-practice); [PDF](https://www.psychologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:658d39fe-2f4b-467f-9ae2-ffeb25caf2b6/McNamara.PsychSci.25.2014.pdf)
- Critique: Ericsson-side reviewers argue Macnamara's definition of deliberate practice was too broad (included domain experience, competition); a reanalysis excluding some effect sizes found accumulated practice explained 29–61% of variance after attenuation correction; a 2019 Frontiers review cites 14% for music. [MODERATE, contested] — [Frontiers 2019 review](https://frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full)
- Platz, Kopiez, Lehmann & Wolf (2014, Frontiers), first music-only meta-analysis, 13 studies: corrected correlation **r = 0.61** between deliberate practice and music achievement. Dispute: Hambrick et al. read it as ~30–36% of variance and argue r = .61 leaves room for other predictors of similar size; Platz et al. object to that variance interpretation. [MODERATE] — [Platz et al. 2014](https://dx.doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00751); [Hambrick et al. "Facing facts"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4101876/)
- Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009), 17 advanced piano majors: total practice time and number of repetitions did **not** predict next-day performance; what predicted it was locating the exact error, slowing/isolating it, and repeating until correct (fixing errors rather than accumulating reps). [MODERATE, small n] — [Bulletproof Musician episode / summary](https://bulletproofmusician.buzzsprout.com/2101894/episodes/18419648-the-central-strategy-expert-musicians-use-to-practice-difficult-passages); [Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)
- Killion & Duke (2025) on artist-level musicians: adding repetitions without correction accumulates incorrect repetitions; experts target the difficult passage precisely. [MODERATE] — [Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.buzzsprout.com/2101894/episodes/18419648-the-central-strategy-expert-musicians-use-to-practice-difficult-passages)
- Gebrian (Learn Faster, Perform Better, OUP 2024): "simply playing through a piece is not practising." [ANECDOTAL/expert synthesis] — [Interlude review](https://interlude.hk/how-neuroscience-helps-musicians-learn-faster-perform-better/)

### Inferences
- The coach should log *what kind* of practice happened (targeted fix / run-through / jamming / learning new material), not just minutes. Minutes are a weak progress proxy.
- Ericsson's core components that transfer to self-learners without a teacher: specific sub-goal per block, immediate feedback (recording, metronome, tab comparison), working just beyond current ability, and error-focused repetition. Self-learners lack a teacher's feedback, so recording-based self-assessment substitutes (see §6).
- Run-throughs/jamming have value (motivation, performance rehearsal) but should be labeled separately from deliberate blocks.

### Gaps
- No studies of deliberate practice in adult hobbyist guitarists, or of rock/metal genres.
- Contribution of practice vs. talent/age-of-start for adult beginners specifically not established.

## 2. Interleaving / contextual interference (CI) and spacing in motor learning and music

### Takeaway
Interleaving (random/rotating practice) tends to hurt in-session performance but improve next-day retention/transfer, with a small-to-moderate effect that is larger in lab tasks and adults than in applied settings. Music evidence is thin and mixed but leans positive. Spacing has a strong verbal-learning base, but one piano study found **no** short-lag spacing effect; overnight sleep between sessions does help.

### Cited Findings
**General motor learning**
- Brady (2004) meta-analysis, 61 studies / 139 effect sizes: overall CI effect ~0.38; basic/lab research 0.57 vs. applied 0.19; adults 0.50 vs. children 0.10. [STRONG for existence, MODERATE for size] — [Brady 2004 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf)
- Jiménez Díaz et al. (2016) meta-analysis of RCTs: blocked practice better in acquisition but worse at retention (ES = −0.25); random practice retention change ES = 0.12 (n.s.). [MODERATE] — [Revista UCR](https://archivo.revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/pem/article/view/23830)
- Czyż et al. (2024) meta-analysis on transfer: random practice favoured in adults (SMD = 0.54) and older adults (SMD = 1.28), negligible in young (SMD = 0.12). Critique (Ammar & Schöllhorn 2025): many low-quality studies, possible publication bias. [MODERATE] — [Frontiers 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1377122/full); [Springer 2025 critique](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10043-1)
- Shea & Morgan (1979): interleaved practice gave better retention at 10 min and 10 days; Hall et al. (1994) elite baseball batters improved 56.7% interleaved vs. 24.8% blocked vs. 6.2% control. Kornell & Bjork (2008): 78% of learners rated blocked equal/better even though 78% did better after interleaving (learners misjudge). [STRONG for the "feels worse, works better" metacognitive illusion] — cited in [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)

**Music-specific**
- Carter & Grahn (2016, Frontiers): 10 advanced clarinetists, within-subjects; blocked = 12 min continuous per piece; interleaved = 3-min alternation between two pieces, 12 min per piece; ~90 min practice day 1, retention test day 2. Interleaved scores averaged higher but were significant only for some raters (e.g., concertos Rater 3 Wilcoxon p = .02; technical studies Day1→Day2 Rater 1 p = .02, all raters combined p = .07). Inter-rater correlations were low (r = 0.02–0.59). Blocked technical studies lost ground overnight. 6/10 judged interleaving most useful but 6/10 *preferred* blocked; all 8 commenters said it improved focus, goal-setting and error-spotting. [MODERATE-weak: n = 10] — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Stambaugh (2011, JRME 58(4)): 41 beginning clarinetists, three 7-pitch exercises; random-order practice produced faster playing at retention; no difference at end of practice; no accuracy or attitude differences. [MODERATE] — [Georgia Southern repository](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/music-facpubs/2)
- Other Stambaugh work (as summarised by Carter & Grahn): Stambaugh & Demorest (2010) found no schedule effect on accuracy for 7th-graders; Stambaugh (2011a) found the effect in university woodwind players but **not** brass players. [MODERATE, mixed] — [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Wong (2022, honours thesis), 21 pianists memorising: interleaved slightly better than blocked but not significant. [weak] — [UWO repository](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/psychd_uht/42)
- Abushanab & Bishara (2013, Memory & Cognition, "Memory and metacognition for piano melodies"): from my recall, random-order practice of melodies gave better retention while participants *predicted* fixed-order would be better. **Not verified in this session** — search did not retrieve the paper. [UNVERIFIED]
- Spacing in piano: Wiseheart, D'Souza & Chae (2017, PLoS One), novices learning a 17-note sequence with 0/1/5/10/15-min lags: **no spacing effect** found. [MODERATE, null] — [PMC5553926](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5553926/); [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/11/09-1)
- Simmons (2012, JRME 59(4)) "Distributed Practice and Procedural Memory Consolidation in Musicians' Skill Learning" exists; results not retrieved. — [citation via PMC5553926](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5553926/)
- Gebrian recommends combining blocked practice (for initial acquisition) with interleaving; interleaving's benefit is in retention/transfer, not in how good it sounds during the session. [ANECDOTAL/expert] — [The Strad](https://www.thestrad.com/four-alternative-methods-to-make-sure-your-practice-efforts-survive-the-pressures-of-performance/7034.article)

### Inferences
- Coach rule: use **blocked** practice for the very first encounter with a new lick/technique (until it can be played correctly at a slow tempo), then switch to **interleaved** rotation (e.g., 3–5 min per item, cycling through 2–4 items) for consolidation. This matches the Carter & Grahn protocol and Gebrian's advice.
- Warn the user that interleaved sessions will *feel* worse; judge by next-day performance (the app should test at the start of the next session).
- Spacing across days (via sleep) is better-supported for music than minute-level spacing within a session.

### Gaps
- No guitar-specific CI or spacing experiments found.
- No data on optimal number of items to interleave or block length for guitar; 3 min comes from one study.
- Effects measured over 1 day; long-term (weeks) music data lacking.

## 3. Speed building: slow vs. at-tempo, metronome ladders, bursts, chunking, mental practice

### Takeaway
There is no experimental evidence that settles slow vs. fast practice for guitar. Slow practice is ubiquitous and helps with learning note sequences/information load; Troy Grady's position (as reported) is that fast motions are often mechanically different, so they must be found/tested at speed. A hybrid (slow for sequence learning, short at/above-tempo bursts for motion discovery) is the best-supported synthesis. Mental practice has a real but small-to-moderate effect.

### Cited Findings
- A qualitative study found musicians perceive slow practice as managing information load and building a motor-learning foundation; a questionnaire study found slow practice extremely common but not clearly linked to expertise. [MODERATE-weak] — [Bulletproof Musician search summary / Psychology of Music 2022](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/03057356221129650); [Maxfield slow practice PDF](https://www.vocology.utah.edu/_resources/documents/slow_practice_maxfield.pdf)
- Bulletproof Musician (Jason Sulliman episode) argues fast/at-tempo practice can be more efficient than slow practice; Graham Fitch episode presents slow practice alongside chaining and interleaving. [ANECDOTAL] — [Bulletproof Musician feed](https://bulletproofmusician.buzzsprout.com/2101894/episodes/17800832-graham-fitch-on-playful-practice-and-muscle-memory-you-can-trust)
- Troy Grady (Cracking the Code) community interpretation: the movement that works at speed must be discovered through experimentation at speed; "the way you play fast might require a different way of playing than when you play slower"; intricate note sequences may still need slow learning. **I could not retrieve Grady's own primary statement**; these are forum/secondary readings. [ANECDOTAL] — [Troy Grady forum: Slow vs Fast Practice](https://forum.troygrady.com/t/slow-vs-fast-practice/31293); [TheFretboard thread](https://thefretboard.co.uk/discussion/comment/2404824); [Speed bursts thread](https://forum.troygrady.com/t/getting-faster-are-speed-bursts-effective/32574/51)
- Speed bursts: users report playing a short fragment as fast as possible then resting improved speed more than slow metronome work; others worry about reinforcing sloppiness. Shawn Lane reportedly recommended working chunks from both slowest and fastest tempos; Petrucci's method reported as raising tempo gradually until errors appear. [ANECDOTAL] — [forum: systematic improvement](https://forum.troygrady.com/t/how-do-did-you-systematically-improve-your-guitar-game/5063); [Shawn Lane thread](https://forum.troygrady.com/t/shawn-lane-on-developing-speed/1692)
- Duke et al. (2009): top pianists slowed down and isolated the error spot, then gradually restored tempo; correct repetitions before moving on predicted next-day success. [MODERATE] — [Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)
- Mental practice: Feltz & Landers (1983), 60 studies / 146 effect sizes, average ES = 0.48 vs. control; a 2018-era replication of Driskell et al. (1994) with 37 studies found a small positive effect (r = 0.131), moderated by duration, task type, imagery type; 2023 meta-analysis compared physical vs. mental vs. combined practice (combined generally best per the literature; specific values not retrieved). [STRONG that it helps, effect small–moderate; less than physical practice] — [UL replication](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/does-mental-practice-still-enhance-performance-a-24-year-follow-u/); [NAP chapter on Feltz & Landers](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/778/chapter/4); [JPEHM 2023 meta-analysis](https://revistas.uma.es/index.php/JPEHM/en/article/view/17875)

### Inferences (coach rules; no study defines BPM increment rules)
- **No peer-reviewed source specifies a BPM increment.** Common pedagogical conventions (ANECDOTAL, not verified here): +2–5 BPM steps, or ~5% of current tempo. Recommended agent rule: raise by ~3–5% (min +2 BPM) only after **3–5 consecutive clean reps** (or a clean recorded take) at the current tempo; on two failed attempts, drop back to the last clean tempo; never raise more than ~2–3 steps in a single session for the same item, because next-day retention, not in-session peak, is the measure.
- Track three tempos per exercise: *clean tempo* (reliable), *burst tempo* (short fragment, can play briefly), *target tempo*. Add short **speed bursts** (2–4 note-group fragments, 5–10 s, at/above target, with rest) as a minority of time (~20–30% of a speed block), while the majority stays at clean tempo + small increments. This hybrid reconciles Grady-type motion discovery with error-correction evidence.
- Chunking: break a lick into the smallest unit that contains the difficulty, master it, then chain (forward or backward); Duke-style targeting supports isolating the error location.
- Mental practice: suggest it as a low-cost add-on (e.g., visualising a solo away from the guitar, or during rest breaks/injury), never a replacement.
- Next-session "cold" test (first attempt, no warm-up on that item) is the true tempo benchmark the app should store.

### Gaps
- No controlled study on guitar picking speed training methods, metronome ladder parameters, or burst practice.
- Grady's primary claims not verified from his own material in this session.

## 4. Retrieval practice, sleep consolidation, session length, microbreaks

### Takeaway
Sleep between sessions improves motor-sequence accuracy in musicians (moderate evidence). Short rest breaks during early learning produce performance gains, but whether they are true consolidation is now disputed. Retrieval (testing) is well established in verbal learning; in music its analogue is cold play-throughs / playing from memory at session start.

### Cited Findings
- Simmons & Duke (2006, JRME 54(3)): trained musicians (non-pianists) learning a 12-note keyboard melody with the non-dominant hand; those who slept between sessions improved significantly in accuracy; speed/evenness gains varied; morning vs. evening practice similar, suggesting overnight consolidation mattered more than time of day. [MODERATE, secondary summary] — [nkoda blog summary](https://www.nkoda.com/blog/Sleep); [citation listing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/rem-sleep-is-not-committed-to-memory/693B6952D191B34BF1C08D0D312DA07F); [UT thesis "Procedural memory consolidation in musicians"](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2152/3172)
- Bönstrup et al. (2019, Current Biology): during early sequence learning, performance gains occurred during seconds-long rest breaks ("micro-offline gains"), predicted by frontoparietal beta activity; NIH authors argued rest "early and often" may be as critical as practice. 2020 crowdsourced follow-up (N = 951) supported it. [MODERATE] — [Bönstrup & Censor 2019 PDF](https://people.socsci.tau.ac.il/mu/nitzancensor/files/2022/02/BonstrupCensor2019.pdf); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/rest-learning-memory-11073/)
- Critique (2025): micro-offline gains also occur with random (unlearnable) sequences and are partly preplanning/fatigue recovery; "not a valid indicator of offline consolidation." [MODERATE, contradicts above] — [PMC12595466](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12595466/)
- Gebrian: the brain learns through spaced effort and strategic rest (podcast summary). [ANECDOTAL] — [Piano Pod](https://thepianopod.substack.com/p/new-episode-alert-unlocking-the-brain)

### Inferences
- Microbreaks: regardless of mechanism, short rests (10–30 s between reps; a few minutes every ~15–25 min) improve performance during practice and reduce fatigue/overuse load. Encode them; don't claim they "consolidate" learning.
- Prefer **daily shorter sessions** over one long weekly session, because each night of sleep is a consolidation opportunity (Simmons & Duke); for a hobbyist, e.g. 5×30 min > 1×150 min.
- Retrieval analogue: start each session with a **cold run** of the previous session's target items (no warm-up on them), log the result. This both tests retention and provides retrieval practice.
- Session length: no music study found that sets an optimal length. Common expert advice (ANECDOTAL) is focused blocks of ~20–30 min with breaks; total sessions of 30–90 min suit hobbyists.

### Gaps
- No study found on optimal session length or daily practice cap for amateur musicians.
- Retrieval practice (testing effect) specifically for instrumental motor skills not found in this session.

## 5. Goal setting, motivation, habit formation; dangers of over-ambitious deadlines

### Takeaway
If-then plans ("implementation intentions") reliably improve follow-through (d ≈ 0.65). Habits take highly variable time (median ~66 days, range 18–254) and missing a single day does not derail them. Process goals (what to do in practice) are more controllable than outcome deadlines.

### Cited Findings
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006, Adv. Exp. Soc. Psych. 38:69–119): implementation intentions ("If situation X, then I will do Y") had a mean effect d = 0.65 on goal attainment across 94 tests (figure from secondary sources; not checked against original text). [STRONG] — [Konstanz record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f); [stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/gollwitzer-2006-implementation-intentions-and/); [Behavioral Scientist glossary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions)
- Lally et al. (2010, EJSP): 96 volunteers performed a daily behaviour in the same context for 84 days; median 66 days to reach 95% of automaticity plateau, range 18–254 (based on 39 well-fitting participants; upper values extrapolated). Lally herself says the time is "highly variable." [MODERATE] — [Behavioral Scientist analysis](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit); [Surrey interview](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally); [replication proposal](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/download/t_recommendations.reply_pdf.8382af6b73bdd0f7.6d616e757363726970745f7072657072696e742e706466.pdf)
- Carter & Grahn participants reported interleaving improved goal-setting and focus. [weak] — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Focal dystonia literature links time pressure, anxiety and perfectionism (plus sudden increases in practice) with risk — relevant to over-ambitious deadlines (see §6). [MODERATE-weak] — [Rochester review](https://esm.rochester.edu/iml/prjc/poly/?p=1320); [TUM thesis](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1761300/1761300.pdf)

### Inferences
- Goal hierarchy for the agent: long-term outcome goal (e.g., "play Song X solo at 100% tempo") → monthly milestones (e.g., 80% tempo clean) → weekly process goals ("4 sessions × 15 min on the sweep section, ladder from 90 BPM") → per-session micro-goals.
- Use implementation intentions at onboarding and weekly review: "After dinner on Mon/Wed/Fri, I'll practice in the bedroom for 30 min." Let the user set cue + time.
- Treat streaks forgivingly (Lally showed single misses don't matter much); avoid punishing streak resets.
- Deadline realism: estimate tempo progress from logged history (rolling trend of clean tempo) rather than linear user guesses; flag deadlines that require faster tempo gains than history supports, and flag sudden increases in weekly practice volume.
- SMART framing is reasonable (specific, measurable via BPM/accuracy/recording), but no music-specific SMART study was found.

### Gaps
- No studies on goal-setting specifically in adult hobby musicians found; SMART and process-vs-outcome evidence here comes from general/sport psychology and was not retrieved in this session (e.g., Locke & Latham not verified).
- No quantitative data on dropout causes in adult self-taught guitarists.

## 6. Self-assessment, recording, rubrics; injury prevention (tendinitis, focal dystonia, warm-up)

### Takeaway
Self-recording is a practical substitute for teacher feedback, but even experts rate performances inconsistently, so rubrics should be simple and criteria-specific. Guitarists report high rates of playing-related pain (~61% in one survey), and failure to warm up is the most consistently reported (observational) risk factor; focal dystonia is rare (~1% of professionals) but linked to intensive, rapidly increased practice and perfectionism.

### Cited Findings
- Expert raters show only moderate agreement (Thompson & Williamon 2003); in Carter & Grahn, inter-rater r ranged 0.02–0.59. [MODERATE] — [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Rigg et al. (2003), survey of 261 rock/blues/jazz/folk guitarists (US/Canada): 61.3% reported playing-related pain, fretting hand most common; failure to warm up was the only variable significantly associated with injury. [MODERATE, self-report] — [University of Iowa protocol](https://iowaprotocols.medicine.uiowa.edu/protocols/guitar-playing-part-1-health-considerations-music-and-medicine-monday)
- Work (2024) cross-sectional study of guitarists: high injury rate; failure to warm up a risk factor. [MODERATE, observational] — [IOS Press](https://content.iospress.com:443/articles/work/wor230701); [PMC10851889](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10851889); [Guitar World summary](https://www.guitarworld.com/features/new-study-musculoskeletal-injuries)
- Small RCT of a 2-week warm-up in conservatoire students (9 vs. 7) found no significant effect on pain; authors note lack of empirical warm-up research for musicians. [weak/null] — summarised in search results; [Iowa protocol](https://iowaprotocols.medicine.uiowa.edu/protocols/guitar-playing-part-1-health-considerations-music-and-medicine-monday)
- Musicians' PRMD prevalence: up to 90% 12-month prevalence in classical musicians; 43–63% students, >80% professionals (Portnoy et al. 2022 summary). [MODERATE] — [PMC10851889](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10851889)
- Focal dystonia: ~1–2% of professional musicians (vs. ~0.03% general population); guitarists and pianists among highest incidence. Systematic review (2016, 16 studies, 1,144 musicians, evidence quality intermediate–poor): intensive playing is a risk factor. Pianists who later developed it increased daily practice more than peers. Other factors: genetic predisposition, overuse, time pressure, anxiety, perfectionism, prior nerve/soft-tissue injury. [MODERATE-weak] — [PMC4736554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736554); [Rochester review](https://esm.rochester.edu/iml/prjc/poly/?p=1320); [MDS abstract](https://www.mdsabstracts.org/?p=16057); [TUM thesis](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1761300/1761300.pdf)

### Inferences
- Recording rule: record a take of each target item at least weekly (and at milestone checks); self-rate with a 3–5 criterion rubric scored 1–5: timing vs. click, note accuracy/clean fretting, string noise/muting, articulation (bends/vibrato pitch, pick attack evenness), musical feel/dynamics. Compare against previous takes rather than absolute judgment (raters are unreliable even among experts).
- Warm-up: always schedule 3–5 min of easy, sub-max-tempo playing at the start; low cost, observational support.
- Injury guardrails for the agent: cap weekly volume increases (e.g., ≤ ~10–20% per week — ANECDOTAL, borrowed from sports-load conventions, not music-validated); insert breaks; ask about pain; if pain persists or loss of fine control/involuntary curling appears in a specific pattern (dystonia warning signs), stop speed work and recommend a medical/musician-health specialist. Never advise "playing through pain."
- Avoid encouraging extreme repetition of the same fast pattern for long periods, especially under deadline pressure.

### Gaps
- No RCT evidence that warm-ups prevent guitar injuries.
- No guitar-specific focal dystonia prevalence data; no validated rubric for rock/metal guitar self-assessment found.

## 7. Concrete rules for an AI practice coach (synthesis)

### Takeaway
Encode: error-focused targeted practice, blocked→interleaved progression, daily spaced sessions with sleep in between, cold-start retention tests as the tempo benchmark, conservative tempo increments gated by clean reps, warm-up + microbreaks + volume caps, and implementation-intention-based scheduling. All allocation numbers below are design heuristics derived from the evidence above, not study-validated values.

### Cited Findings
- Interleaving block of 3 min per item over ~12 min per item worked in Carter & Grahn. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Targeted, error-fixing repetitions predict next-day performance better than total time. — [Structural Learning briefing on Duke et al.](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)
- Sleep between sessions improves accuracy. — [nkoda summary of Simmons & Duke](https://www.nkoda.com/blog/Sleep)
- Failure to warm up associated with guitar injury. — [Iowa protocol](https://iowaprotocols.medicine.uiowa.edu/protocols/guitar-playing-part-1-health-considerations-music-and-medicine-monday)

### Inferences (proposed rules)
**Session templates** (warm-up and review always present; active goals limited to avoid dilution):
- **30 min** (max 2 active goals): 3–5 min warm-up → 3–5 min cold retention check of last session's items → 15–18 min focus block on primary goal (blocked if new, interleaved 3–5 min rotations if consolidating) → 5 min secondary goal or interleaved review → 1–2 min log/rate.
- **60 min** (max 3 active goals): 5 min warm-up → 5 min cold check → 2 × 15 min deep blocks (primary/secondary goal) with 1–2 min break between → 10 min interleaved rotation of older/maintenance items → 5 min musical application (play-along/improvise over backing track using the technique) → log.
- **90 min** (max 4 active goals): as 60 min, plus a 15–20 min repertoire/song block with a recorded take, and a 5 min break at ~45 min. Suggest splitting into two sessions (e.g., 2×45) on different times of day when possible.
- Allocation heuristic: ~50% to the primary (most important/least mastered) goal, ~25% secondary, ~15% review/maintenance, ~10% warm-up + log. Approx. 60–70% "new/hard" material, 30–40% review.

**Tempo rules:** raise target tempo by ~3–5% (≥2 BPM) after 3–5 consecutive clean reps; cap at 2–3 increases per item per session; record new "clean tempo" only if confirmed the **next day cold**; if cold tempo falls >5–10% below yesterday's best, hold or drop and focus on accuracy; include short speed bursts (≤25% of the speed block) above target.

**Stage progression per item:** Learn (blocked, slow, chunked) → Stabilise (clean at ≥60–70% target; begin interleaving) → Speed (ladder + bursts) → Apply (in song/improvisation context) → Maintain (spaced review: e.g., 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 days — an expanding schedule borrowed from verbal spacing research, not music-validated).

**Review cadence:** daily cold check of yesterday's items; weekly review (recording + rubric, compare to last week, adjust goals; re-state implementation intention); monthly milestone check against outcome goal with deadline realism from tempo trend.

**Safety:** mandatory warm-up; microbreaks every 15–25 min; pain check-in prompts; weekly volume increase cap; dystonia/tendinitis red-flag guidance pointing to professionals.

**Motivation:** if-then scheduling; forgiving streaks; celebrate process goals hit; keep a fun block (song/jam) in every session ≥30 min.

### Gaps
- None of the time splits, BPM increment sizes, or review intervals above are validated by guitar or music studies; they are reasoned defaults that the app should treat as tunable and ideally evaluate against the user's own logged data.
